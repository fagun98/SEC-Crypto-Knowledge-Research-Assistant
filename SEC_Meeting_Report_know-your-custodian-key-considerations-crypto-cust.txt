1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
- Purpose: The SEC Crypto Task Force held a two-panel roundtable to explore how SEC custody rules (and related frameworks) should apply to crypto assets — focusing first on broker-dealer custody (Panel 1) and then on investment adviser / investment company custody (Panel 2).
- High-level outcomes: Strong, cross‑cutting agreement that (a) investor protection is paramount; (b) industry needs clear, technology‑neutral, principles‑based standards rather than brittle, prescriptive technical rules; (c) the SEC should provide greater clarity about who qualifies as a "qualified custodian" (including whether state‑chartered trust companies and other licenced entities qualify); (d) self‑custody and emerging custody models (MPC, smart‑contract arrangements, proof‑of‑reserves, Merkle trees) can have a role but require safeguards, audits and disclosure; and (e) insolvency/resolution protections (SIPA, custody/credit protections, UCC Article 8) remain unresolved and may require interagency work and possibly legislative action.
- No rulemaking decision was announced. The roundtable solicited perspectives to inform the Commission’s next steps and encouraged written submissions and continued engagement.

3. VIEWS ON TOPICS
Main topics below show viewpoints expressed, level of consensus and remaining disagreement.

Topic A — Overarching regulatory framing / custody rule baseline
- Viewpoints:
  - Chairman/SEC staff: existing custody rules are high‑bar; any change must preserve investor protection and consider bank / qualified custodian concepts, insolvency protection (SIPA), and systemic risks. (Chairman Atkins repeated throughout.) [Transcript]
  - Many industry panelists: Seek clarity and a principles‑based, technology‑neutral standard so innovation isn't stifled and rules do not become obsolete. (Jason, Tammy, Mark, Rachel) [Transcript]
- Consensus: Clear need for clarity and for technology‑neutral, principles‑based standards rather than prescriptive technical requirements.
- Disagreement: Degree of how prescriptive (some argued for clearer entity/charter recognition; others favored high‑level principles plus supervisory guidance).

Topic B — Who qualifies as a "Qualified Custodian" (banks, state trust companies, other licenses)
- Viewpoints:
  - Banks / federally chartered custodians: Many argued these fit the historical model that the rule relied on and provide bankruptcy remoteness and supervisor oversight (Rachel, Chairman Atkins).
  - State chartered trust companies / specialized charters: Several panelists (Baylor, Brandon, others) urged formal SEC clarity recognizing state trust charters and other specialized charters as QC if they meet defined standards.
  - Technology firms / MTLs / nonbank licensees: Panelists cautioned against automatically accepting low‑threshold licenses (e.g., simple money transmitter licenses) as sufficient; suggested objective standard‑setting (policies, audits, capital, examinations) rather than only license labels.
- Consensus: Need for Commission clarity and for a framework that permits competition while ensuring safety.
- Disagreement: Whether the SEC should limit QC to bank/broker dealer types or formally expand to include state trust charters and selected nontraditional licensees under conditions.

Topic C — Principles‑based / technology‑neutral standards vs prescriptive rules
- Viewpoints:
  - Majority supported a principles‑based approach: avoid mandating specific technologies (MPC vs multisig, hot/cold ratios) because technology evolves rapidly (Jason, Tammy, Mark, Rachel).
  - Some stressed the immediate commercial need for clarity even if limited: "clarity, not more regulation." (Baylor) — industry wants near‑term, actionable guidance.
- Consensus: Principles + objective standards (audits, controls, disclosure) favored over prescriptive technology rules.
- Disagreement: Some urged clearer expectations for audits and independent QC recognition earlier rather than long, slow rulemaking.

Topic D — Omnibus wallets vs segregated wallets; netting/movement tradeoffs
- Viewpoints:
  - Pro‑omnibus (netting / less movement): Mark Greenberg and others argued that omnibus netting reduces on‑chain movement and lowers exploit risk. They favor operational models that reduce asset movement.
  - Pro‑segregation / transparency: Others highlighted transparency and verifiability advantages of segregated wallets and proof‑of‑reserves techniques (Merkle trees), but acknowledged operational cost and liquidity tradeoffs.
- Consensus: No single right answer — tradeoffs exist; disclosure and standards must reflect chosen architecture. Audits and cryptographic proofs can aid omnibus models.
- Disagreement: Whether omnibus wallets should be allowed generally for broker‑dealer custody of crypto securities (Chairman and others flagged increased risk).

Topic E — Proof of exclusive control / audits / "How do you prove a negative?"
- Viewpoints:
  - Industry: You can’t literally "prove a negative" re: private key copies, but control can be demonstrated by audited key ceremonies, contracts, operational evidence, on‑chain verification, and independent SOC / code audits (Terrence, Jason, BitGo).
  - Regulators: Want clarity as to what evidence suffices; suggested audits, contracts, third‑party verification will be important.
- Consensus: Audits (SOC/crypto‑specific), contracts and on‑chain proofs (where applicable) are viable evidence buckets; regulators should define acceptable attestations.
- Disagreement: None fundamental; more discussion around which types of audits/reports suffice.

Topic F — Self‑custody for advisers / funds vs third‑party qualified custody
- Viewpoints:
  - Pro‑self‑custody (conditional): Several argued advisers with capability should be allowed, particularly where no qualified custodian exists for particular asset types — with strict safeguards, disclosure and exams (Neil, Chuck, Ryan, Larry).
  - Caution (keep qualified custodian preference): Some advocates of QC caution about insolvency/examination/resolution differences and argued QC retains important investor protections (Susan, Rachel).
- Consensus: Self‑custody can be an option for advisers in some circumstances but must be accompanied by strong, principles‑based controls, audits, disclosure and regulator awareness.
- Disagreement: Extent and conditions of safe self‑custody; whether regulators should require QC in all cases.

Topic G — Staking, DeFi, tokenized securities, smart-contract custody
- Viewpoints:
  - Staking and active, productive uses are economically valuable; advisers may need to engage to fulfill fiduciary duty (Justin Browder’s argument: duty to pursue client interests may require staking).
  - Custody rules struggle to accommodate staking because staking often requires moving assets on‑chain (which may not be compatible with "custody" as historically framed); some suggested safe harbors or bespoke accommodations.
  - Smart‑contract custody: Possible but functionally someone (an address/key) will control actions — that party becomes the effective custodian; immutable/disposable keys raise operational and governance questions.
- Consensus: Staking/delegation/DeFi pose unique custody challenges; regulators should consider targeted relief or clarifying guidance; technological solutions (MPC, tokenized staking-as-a-service) are evolving and could be accommodated under principles‑based rules.
- Disagreement: Whether staking must be foreclosed for advisers under custody rules or accommodated by non‑exclusive safe harbors.

Topic H — Insolvency/resolution protections (SIPA, customer protections, UCC Article 8)
- Viewpoints:
  - Many panelists called out gaps in SIPA/CIPC protections when assets are not securities or when custody models differ from traditional custody. Some suggested Congress may need to act to extend SIPA‑like protections to certain crypto assets.
  - Several recommended explicit adoption / encouragement of UCC Article 8 (or new Article 12) provisions to clarify entitlements and perfection by control; Chuck Mooney in particular advocated Article 8 / Article 12 adoption.
- Consensus: Insolvency/resolution protections are a major unresolved policy question and require interagency and/or legislative work.
- Disagreement: Some stressed the need for immediate SEC staff guidance; others thought Congressional action will be necessary for SIPA changes.

4. MEETING CONCLUSION
Overall outcome:
- No final rule or change was adopted at the meeting. The SEC used the roundtable to gather inputs and public perspectives from operators, custodians, counsel, academics, and fund managers to inform possible next steps.
- The Commission emphasized investor protection as the North Star, insisted on preserving custody rule's protective intent, and signaled openness to exploring qualified custodian definitions, technology‑neutral principles, and interagency coordination.

Action items / next steps mentioned or implied:
- SEC invited continued input: public written submissions to the Crypto Task Force (Chair and staff explicitly asked for written input and meetings) and public participation in future roundtables (tokenization roundtable upcoming). (Transcript: closing remarks encouraging comments and use of the crypto task force website.)
- The staff will consider:
  - Clarifying who may qualify as a qualified custodian (possible recognition of state trust charters and specified firms that meet standards), and whether “QC” can be defined by objective standards.
  - Developing principles‑based standards and supervisory / exam expectations (audits, documentation, contracts, KYC/AML, controls, key generation/audit trails) that can be applied regardless of underlying technology.
  - Exploring interactions with SIPA/insolvency and UCC Article 8 (or Article 12) for clarity on entitlements and recovery in failures.
  - Considering how staking and smart‑contract custodial models could be addressed (either by safe harbors, guidance or rule changes).

Key takeaways for practitioners / stakeholders:
- Prepare concrete, written submissions illustrating how different custody models can achieve the SEC’s investor protection goals (audits, proof of control, contractual safekeeping promises, independent custody structures, resolution arrangements).
- Expect the SEC to favor technology‑neutral, principles‑based outcomes accompanied by objective, demonstrable controls (audits, reporting, independent verification).
- Insolvency/resolution gaps (SIPA, purchaser protections) may require legislative / interagency attention — firms should engage with policymakers as well as the SEC.

7. RECOMMENDATIONS (inferred from meeting + common regulatory practice)
- SEC should work toward an explicit, public, technology‑neutral set of principles and supervisory expectations for crypto custody, including:
  - Objective evidence buckets that constitute "control" (contracts with safekeeping promise, audited key ceremonies and SOC/crypto audits, on‑chain proofs where available).
  - Minimum (but technology‑neutral) controls: segregation/recordkeeping, third‑party audits, independent verification, capital/resolution considerations for custodians, surprise exams where applicable.
  - A transparent approach to qualifying state‑chartered trust companies and other licenced custodians (set explicit criteria rather than leave reliance on lawyer opinion letters).
  - Targeted guidance on staking and DeFi interactions (e.g., when adviser engagement is permitted and what compensating controls are required).
  - Interagency coordination on insolvency/resolution protections for token assets (SIPA gaps) and consideration of UCC Article 8 / Article 12 adoption.
