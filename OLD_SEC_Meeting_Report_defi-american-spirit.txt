1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
- Event: SEC Crypto Task Force roundtable entitled “DeFi and the American Spirit” (June 9, 2025).
- Purpose: Final roundtable in a series to inform the SEC’s approach to crypto regulation by focusing on decentralized finance (DeFi) — defining DeFi, identifying where securities/regulation attach, and balancing protection with innovation.
- Format: Opening remarks by SEC leadership (Chairman and Commissioners), moderated panel (Troy Paradus), multi‑disciplinary panelists (industry technologists, entrepreneurs, academics, investors), audience Q&A. On-the-record, livestreamed.
- High‑level outcome: No binding regulatory pronouncements; Chairman Atkins announced staff-level direction to explore an “innovation exemption” (conditional exemptive relief) and directed staff to examine fit‑for‑purpose rulemaking and accommodations for on‑chain markets. The roundtable surfaced a set of recurring policy tradeoffs and concrete technical/legal issues that the SEC and industry need to address jointly (definitions, custody, activity‑based regulation, disclosure vs. fraud enforcement, stablecoins, interoperability, privacy/identity solutions). Multiple participants urged rulemaking that is careful, legally grounded, and informed by technical realities.

3. VIEWS ON TOPICS
(For each main topic: succinct summary of perspectives and where consensus/disagreement emerged.)

Topic A — How to define DeFi and decentralization
- Viewpoints:
  - Rebecca: practical, activity‑based definition — DeFi as “blockchain‑based open software system” enabling self‑directed, self‑custodial transactions.
  - Jill: DeFi is now modular (not monolithic); focus on where control sits across layers.
  - Omid/Kevin/Gabe: prefer functional, architecture and trust‑oriented views.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: a single crisp definition is elusive; regulators should focus on activities and where control rests (who exercises power).
  - Disagreement: how much emphasis to put on self‑custody vs. practical, “peer‑to‑pool” reality. Some panelists noted that much DeFi is peer‑to‑pool and intermediate-like behavior emerges.

Topic B — Activities‑based regulation vs. technology/actor‑based rules
- Viewpoints:
  - Rebecca, Michael Jordan, Jill: favor an activities‑based approach (focus on what is being done — custody, execution, order routing).
  - Peter, others: emphasize constitutional limits (First Amendment) on regulating code publication; but accept regulating actors who operate, custody, or administer systems.
  - Some (e.g., regulator speakers) emphasized necessity of rulemaking and legal process to clarify jurisdiction.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: activity‑based approach is practically useful; publication of code alone should not be actionable.
  - Disagreement: degree of legal certainty needed and whether bright‑line rules or principles should be used. Some panelists warned against over‑broad disclosure mandates; others urged targeted disclosure for material off‑chain agreements.

Topic C — Custody / self‑custody and the role of intermediaries
- Viewpoints:
  - Commissioner Pur, Rebecca, Kevin: self‑custody is fundamental; custody status materially changes regulatory posture.
  - Kevin and others: stablecoins (largely custodial) are critical infrastructure and raise systemic and regulatory questions.
  - Some panelists: real‑world mixes exist (partial custody, frontends, multi‑sig actors).
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: custody is a critical determinant of risk and regulatory reach.
  - Disagreement: whether custodial stablecoins should be the primary legal path for on‑chain markets (some saw custody requirement as practical but constraining to DeFi).

Topic D — Code as speech / First Amendment constraints
- Viewpoints:
  - Peter and others strongly argued publication of code is protected speech and regulators should avoid prior restraints; enforcement is appropriate for behavior (operation/custody) not mere publication.
  - Others cautioned that actors often blur lines by operating/maintaining protocols and may be regulable.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: distinct line between publishing code and operating/administrating a service — regulators should be careful before treating pure publication as a regulated act.
  - Disagreement: precise legal tests and how enforcement cases should be framed (e.g., Low v. SEC analogies discussed).

Topic E — Disclosure vs. enforcement (fraud) as regulatory tools
- Viewpoints:
  - Many panelists (Gabe, Michael Jordan, Jill, Rebecca) support targeted disclosure of material off‑chain agreements (market‑maker deals, subsidies, governance powers).
  - Others (Eric, Peter) warned that heavy, formalized disclosure obligations can be costly and stifle innovation; preferred fraud/anti‑misrepresentation enforcement in many cases.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: transparency is valuable; many industry-led disclosure tools already exist (e.g., L2Beat, DeFi dashboards).
  - Disagreement: whether disclosure mandates are the best, least‑cost instrument vs. fraud‑based enforcement and private law remedies.

Topic F — Risks: cyber, governance, illicit finance, systemic scaling
- Viewpoints:
  - Rebecca: cyber risk is primary; system management and illicit finance are additional risks.
  - Kevin, Jill: emphasized operational and privacy risks; suggested tech remedies (zero‑knowledge proofs) and activity‑based detection.
  - Several urged scenario planning for “if DeFi scales to broader capital markets.”
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: cyber/operational risks are central; need for mitigation like audits, safe harbors for white‑hat response, privacy enhancing tech.
  - Disagreement: the level of regulatory intrusion necessary to solve those risks.

Topic G — Stablecoins and interoperability
- Viewpoints:
  - Kevin and others: stablecoins are essential to DeFi; most large stablecoins are custodial and will be regulated — which affects DeFi operation.
  - Jill and others: emphasized interoperability and modular infrastructure as key for scalability and safety.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus: stablecoins are central; their regulatory treatment will materially affect DeFi’s evolution.
  - Disagreement: extent to which custody requirements for legal stablecoins will limit DeFi or drive innovation to alternative (non‑custodial) designs.

4. MEETING CONCLUSION
- Overall outcome:
  - No final rules announced. Major outcome: policy framing and staff direction (Chairman Atkins) to explore a “conditional exemptive relief framework” / “innovation exemption” and to consider fit‑for‑purpose rule amendments. Commissioners reiterated need for careful rulemaking and public input.
  - The roundtable functioned as a cross‑stakeholder information gathering and boundary‑setting exercise, surfacing technical issues regulators must consider.
- Action items / next steps mentioned:
  - SEC staff directed to consider a conditional exemptive relief framework to allow on‑chain market experiments subject to conditions (Chairman Atkins; transcript).
  - Continued public engagement: commissioners and task force invited written comments and meetings (crypto task force website crypto.se.gov referenced in transcript).
  - Implicit next steps: (a) staff technical/legal analyses on custody, activities test, and constitutional constraints; (b) scenario planning for large‑scale DeFi adoption (systemic/market structure implications); (c) working with industry on disclosure frameworks and identity/privacy tech (zero‑knowledge proofs).
- Key takeaways:
  1. DeFi is technically complex and heterogeneous; regulatory approaches must recognize a spectrum of decentralization.
  2. Activity‑based regulation (who does what) is pragmatically useful; custody status is a key dividing line.
  3. Constitutional limits (code as speech) matter — publishing code should not be a prior restraint, but operating/administrating can be regulated.
  4. The SEC is actively exploring staff options, including an “innovation exemption,” but will proceed through careful legal processes (notice and comment).
  5. Industry self‑policing, modular technical architectures, and advanced privacy/identity tech (ZK proofs, verifiable credentials) are central tools — regulators should consider technological solutions as part of runbooks.

5. KEY POINTS AND MAIN ARGUMENTS / SIGNIFICANT MOMENTS
(Representative list; direct quotes and paraphrases taken from the transcript)

A. Chairman policy direction (significant moment)
- Quote: “I’ve directed the staff to consider a conditional exemptive relief framework or let's call it an innovation exemption that would expeditiously allow registrants to bring on‑chain products and services to market.” — Chairman Atkins (transcript).
- Significance: concrete staff instruction that will shape near‑term rule/drafting work.

B. Definition & regulatory lens
- Rebecca: clear activity lens — “I typically define DeFi as a blockchain‑based open software system that allows users to engage in economic transactions in a self‑directed and entirely self‑custodial manner...” — argued we should regulate activities, not code publication.
- Significance: shaped much of the panel’s follow‑on discussion.

C. First Amendment and code
- Peter (Coin Center): “Code is protected speech. The SEC must not infringe on First Amendment rights by regulating someone who merely publishes code… If somebody else subsequently violates the law using the software protocol, the user, not the developer of the software, should face the music.” (transcript).
- Significance: legal guardrail against overly broad SEC reach; repeated by multiple panelists.

D. Custody as regulatory hinge
- Commissioner Pur & multiple panelists emphasized non‑custodial/self‑custody as a defining feature of DeFi and a decisive factor for regulatory treatment: “The right to have self‑custody of one's private property is a foundational American value” (transcript).
- Significance: custody status repeatedly raised as one of the strongest bright‑line factors.

E. Activity / risk triage
- Rebecca: highlighted three central risks (cyber, system management, illicit finance) and that they should drive regulatory attention and mitigation strategies (transcript).
- Significance: triage suggests regulators prioritize cyber/operational risk mitigation and rely on newer technical detection solutions.

F. DeFi resilience example (FTX)
- Jill: recounted watching DeFi manage liquidations during FTX collapse — “DeFi stood up … watching DeFi hold up under the pressure … was an extraordinary experience.” (transcript).
- Significance: empirical evidence that permissionless systems can reduce certain centralized failure modes.

G. Disclosure vs fraud debate
- Michael Jordan and Gabe argued for clearer disclosure of off‑chain material arrangements (e.g., market‑maker deals) and for a “golden bridge” for compliant projects; Eric and others warned that onerous disclosures could stifle small innovators.
- Significance: ongoing tension between mandatory disclosure vs. fraud‑based enforcement or industry self‑policing.

H. Stablecoins & systemic scope
- Kevin: stablecoins are essential to DeFi; their regulatory posture (custodial vs non‑custodial) will shape DeFi’s practical fate. He urged scenario planning if DeFi scales to core capital markets.
- Significance: stablecoins are both an enabler and potential regulatory choke point.

I. Technology solutions (privacy & identity)
- Michael Moer / other panelists proposed privacy‑enhancing techniques and zero‑knowledge proofs as ways to verify status (e.g., accredited investor, age) at transaction speed without disclosing PII — a recurring technical remedy to reconcile privacy and compliance.
- Significance: points to technology as part of regulatory solutions.

J. Notable legal case references used to illustrate boundaries
- EtherDelta / Coburn settlement used to show how centralized attributes of a purported DeFi system triggered SEC enforcement (Commissioner Pur referenced this).
- Low v. SEC jurisprudence was discussed as an analogy for publication vs. conduct regulation.
