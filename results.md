Several consistent trends show up in SEC‑related materials that discuss SEC–CFTC overlap and harmonization:

   - The materials repeatedly frame SEC and CFTC jurisdiction as different by statute, but emphasize **coordination and flexibility** so regulation does not block innovation.

2. **Harmonization is increasingly framed as “aligned market plumbing”**
   - A central theme is aligning **capital and margin frameworks** (including **portfolio-based margining**) to reduce duplicative collateral and conflicting compliance/registration burdens, especially where products or strategies span SEC and CFTC markets.
   - Another recurring focus is harmonizing **product and venue definitions** and **streamlining reporting/data standards**—i.e., consistent definitions and data so jurisdiction and compliance obligations are clearer and less duplicative.

3. **Use of targeted exemptions/safe harbors as a practical bridge**
   - The summaries show a trend toward discussing **exemptive relief** and **targeted safe harbors** (including for areas like DeFi) as interim tools to allow experimentation and new models while longer-term rules are developed—especially where overlap or gaps exist.

4. **Overlap/gap mapping is becoming explicit, especially for crypto derivatives and tokenization**
   - Multiple materials explicitly note work on **“jurisdictional overlap” and “jurisdictional gaps”** in the context of **novel derivative crypto products** and **tokenized equities**, with emphasis on definitional alignment (e.g., what constitutes a “facility of an exchange”) to determine which regime applies.

5. **A competitiveness/onshoring rationale appears alongside investor protection**
   - Cross-agency coordination is also described as a way to **onshore activity** (e.g., considering steps related to perpetual contracts) and better align U.S. market structure with global markets, while still emphasizing investor protection and market integrity.

- **Direct-holding infrastructure proposals + broader cost-benefit framing**: advocacy for a more disintermediated model (e.g., a National Public System concept) and a broader cost–benefit analysis that includes wider social-welfare considerations and knock-on effects (margin, securities lending, rehypothecation, AML/sanctions, tax).
