1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
- Purpose: The Crypto Task Force roundtable (Dec. 15, 2025) convened SEC Commissioners, staff, technologists, developers, lawyers and policy advocates to examine financial surveillance and privacy in crypto and related technologies. The discussion aimed to surface technical capabilities (privacy-preserving tools), law enforcement and regulatory interests (AML/CFT, sanctions, market integrity), and policy approaches that balance national security / law enforcement needs with civil liberties and functional capital markets.
- Tone and thrust: Participants broadly agreed that privacy is an important societal value and an economic enabler for moving financial markets on-chain, while acknowledging real risks posed by illicit finance, sanctions evasion, and market manipulation. Technical speakers described mature and emerging privacy technologies (zero-knowledge proofs, selective disclosure, privacy pools, permissioned/segmented architectures) and how those tools can support compliance without wholesale loss of privacy. Policy discussants emphasized the need for careful, nuanced regulation (including sandboxes/exemptive relief), clearer guidance for industry, experimentation, and stronger engagement between regulators and builders.
- Outcome: No rulemaking or formal decisions were announced. The roundtable surfaced concrete policy ideas and next steps (areas of regulatory attention, opportunities for regulators to engage with technologists, possible sandbox/exemptive pathways). Several panelists urged the SEC and other agencies to (a) engage more closely with developers and industry; (b) consider tailored, technology‑aware regulatory approaches (including the potential for no‑action/exemptive relief and sandboxes); and (c) prioritize R&D and standards for privacy-preserving identity and compliance tooling.

3. VIEWS ON TOPICS
(Each topic lists the viewpoints expressed, key speakers for those positions, and whether there was consensus or disagreement.)

Topic: General framing — Privacy vs. Financial Surveillance
- Viewpoints:
  - Chairman Atkins / Commissioners: Privacy is a fundamental American value but regulators have legitimate national security and AML interests; regulators should be humble; don’t create mass surveillance architectures (Atkins).
  - Commissioner Peirce: Privacy should be default; government should not assume ill intent; avoid imposing obligations on non‑custodial software developers.
  - Civil liberties (ACLU / Jay Stanley): Privacy should be protected to guard speech and autonomy; overbroad collection is dangerous.
- Consensus/Disagreements: Broad consensus that privacy is desirable and that policy must be carefully balanced. Differences arise about how aggressively to apply existing AML/Bank Secrecy Act obligations to new technologies (some fear overreach).

Representative quotes:
- “If the instinct of the government is to treat every wallet like a broker … the government will transform this ecosystem into a financial panopticon.” — Chairman Atkins [Transcript]
- “Protecting one's privacy should be the norm, not an indicator of criminal intent.” — Commissioner Hester Peirce [Transcript]

Topic: Privacy‑preserving technologies (Zcash, ZK proofs, mixers, selective disclosure)
- Viewpoints:
  - Builders (Zooko, Jill, Cole, Simon): ZK proofs / selective disclosure / privacy pools enable privacy while preserving auditability or selective compliance; programmability + privacy (“smart privacy”) allows compliance policies to be enforced without wholesale surveillance.
  - Law enforcement / AML policy voices (Carole, others): Acceptance that tech exists but stressed need for supervisory access and alternative risk mitigants; spoke of the need for careful design.
- Consensus/Disagreements: General agreement that technology can provide selective disclosure and programmable compliance; disagreement about how accessible/usable these tools are today for compliance and whether regulators can / should demand certain developer behaviors.
- Representative quotes:
  - “What I think is the most important thing … is agency. It's who chooses what gets shared and what doesn't get shared.” — Zooko Wilcox [Transcript]
  - “Smart privacy … allows business and companies to construct policies that are enforceable on chain so that they can meet their specific risk mitigation requirements.” — Cole (Aleo) [Transcript]

Topic: Tokenized securities, DeFi, and the application of AML/KYC/BSA
- Viewpoints:
  - Nikhil Raghuveera, Simon Letort: Tokenized securities adoption requires privacy and permissionless rails with programmable policy enforcement layers (allowing accreditation, on‑chain policy enforcement). They advocated building on permissionless infrastructure with policy hooks/enforcement “on top.”
  - J.W. Verret, others: Concern that attaching traditional AML/KYC obligations to DeFi/tokenized equity raises statutory and constitutional issues; suggested caution about extending requirements to non‑custodial protocols.
  - Carole House: Practicality and nuance — stronger adoption of existing obligations at the institution level before expanding requirements; attention to where obligations realistically apply.
- Consensus/Disagreements: Agreement that privacy is important to tokenization and that programmable enforcement can help. Disagreement on where to apply AML obligations (user, protocol, or intermediaries) and legal basis for imposing requirements on DeFi.
- Representative quote:
  - “Adoption of tokenized securities requires privacy. It's absolutely essential and it requires permissionless rails.” — Nikhil Raghuveera [Transcript]

Topic: Digital identity and selective disclosure (mobile driver’s licenses, state pilots)
- Viewpoints:
  - Wayne Chang, Linda Jeng: Presented practical pilots (California DMV wallet, Utah state‑endorsed identity) demonstrating privacy-preserving digital credentials and selective disclosure (NIST/NCCoE R&D recommended).
  - Carole, Katherine: Called for robust privacy as infrastructure; recognized identity as key to scalable, privacy‑preserving compliance.
- Consensus/Disagreements: Broad consensus that privacy-preserving digital identities are both necessary and feasible; need policy frameworks (state pilots, NIST) and standardization.
- Representative quote:
  - “We have the policy as well getting figured out so that it is constitutionally based protections for use of digital identity.” — Wayne Chang [Transcript]

Topic: Enforcement, developer protections, and chilling effects
- Viewpoints:
  - Industry / developers: Concerned about prosecutions (examples cited in transcript), lack of developer protections and chilling effects on U.S. on‑shore innovation.
  - Regulators/policy advocates: Varied; some urged better engagement and clear exemptions/sandbox mechanisms to protect innovators.
- Consensus/Disagreements: Agreement that policy uncertainty discourages on‑shore innovation; different views on the remedies (no‑action letters / exemptive relief / executive clemency / clear statutory protections).
- Representative quote:
  - Panel concerns expressed about “culture of fear” that discourages developers from building on U.S. soil; calls for clearer regulatory safe harbors and engagement. [Transcript]

4. MEETING CONCLUSION
- Overall outcome:
  - No formal rule changes, enforcement actions, or immediate policy directives were announced. The roundtable functioned as an information‑gathering and stakeholder engagement event. Important conceptual outcomes include: (a) technical feasibility of privacy + compliance (ZK, selective disclosure, segmented ledgers); (b) regulatory appetite for nuanced approaches rather than blunt, surveillance‑heavy solutions; (c) shared interest in experimentation, sandboxes, and clearer guidance.
- Action items / next steps mentioned (explicit or strongly implied in transcript):
  - Regulators should continue engagement with builders and experiment with technology-neutral sandboxes and exemptive/no‑action relief (several speakers urged this).
  - Agencies (SEC / Treasury / FinCEN / NIST) should coordinate to evaluate privacy‑preserving identity standards (NIST NCCoE work referenced).
  - Industry should produce implementation templates and KPIs for pilot programs that can be assessed by regulators (suggested by several panelists).
  - Consideration of revisiting data collection practices (Chairman Atkins cited CAT experience) and reassessing scopes/cost/utility of centralized data hoarding.
  - Improve clarity on where AML/BSA/KYC obligations apply in DeFi/permissionless environments and potential legislative clarity where necessary.
- Key takeaways (summary bullets):
  - Privacy is both a civil liberty and an economic enabler for on‑chain markets; protecting privacy is not necessarily at odds with regulatory objectives if the right technical and governance approaches are used.
  - Privacy‑preserving cryptography (ZK proofs, multiparty computation, selective disclosure) and segmented ledger architectures provide practical ways to deliver compliance without mass surveillance.
  - For regulated institutions, a segmented/permissioned or selectively disclosed model (where regulators or authorized observers can receive evidence) appears more aligned with supervisory needs than fully replicated public ledgers for many regulated assets.
  - Regulators should foster and enable secure on‑shore innovation by clarifying expectations, enabling experimentation (sandboxes/no‑action/exemptive relief), and considering developer/user protections.
  - Digital identity (privacy preserving) is a central building block for scalable compliance; state pilots and NIST work are relevant models.

Main arguments (for and against principal policy positions):
- For stronger privacy-preserving design:
  - Enables capital formation and institutional adoption (protects trading strategies, payroll secrecy, etc.).
  - Supports civil liberties and individual autonomy.
  - Technologies exist to provide selective disclosure and compliance (ZK proofs, privacy pools).
  - Default privacy reduces chilling effect and can be implemented while preserving regulatory access.
- Against blanket unregulated privacy:
  - Risks of illicit finance, sanctions evasion, and market manipulation if privacy prevents lawful surveillance or investigation.
  - Need for regulators to retain tools to protect national security and financial integrity.
- Reconciling argument:
  - Use programmable privacy and segmented architectures to allow authorized disclosures/supervisory access without mass surveillance; pursue targeted policy that focuses on regulated actors, not indiscriminately on all protocol developers or users.

Recommendations (practical “baby step” actions surfaced by the discussion)
- For the SEC / regulators:
  1. Publish guidance / templates for sandbox/exemptive relief requests (what projects should provide: mitigations, KPIs, monitoring plans, sunset tests).
  2. Increase direct technical engagement: allow regulators and staff hands‑on experimentation (purchase/work with testnet assets, prototypes) to reduce knowledge asymmetry.
  3. Reassess internal data collection: audit major data programs (e.g., CAT) for necessity, retention, and use; prioritize data minimization.
  4. Coordinate with NIST and Treasury on privacy-preserving identity standards (state pilot learnings should be evaluated and disseminated).
  5. Clarify where BSA/AML obligations lawfully attach in DeFi and tokenized securities contexts (statutory analysis and public consultation).
- For industry / builders:
  1. Develop clear, standardized interfaces for selective disclosure / view keys and publish operational best practices for reversible mitigation (e.g., short halting windows, audit trails available to authorized actors).
  2. Aggregate R&D data and KPIs from pilot projects (identity pilots, privacy pools) to share with regulators; propose measurable risk metrics for pilot evaluation.
  3. Form cross‑sector consortia for technical standards (privacy-preserving payment rails, identity attestations), and include civil liberties groups in standard discussions.
- For Congress and policy makers:
  1. Consider statutory clarity where existing laws (BSA/AML) may not be fit for permissionless, non‑custodial protocols — if Congress wants obligations to apply outside traditional intermediaries, explicit legislative authorization is preferable to regulatory reach.
  2. Consider federal baseline personal data rights or incentives for interoperable privacy-preserving interoperable identity standards (recognizing state pilots).
