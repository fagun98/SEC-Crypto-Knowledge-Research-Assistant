1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
- Purpose: The SEC Crypto Task Force’s final spring roundtable examined decentralized finance (DeFi): definitions, risks, when securities/other SEC rules apply, and how regulation could enable innovation while protecting investors.
- Tone & thrust: Commissioners and the Chair framed DeFi as an innovation aligned with “American spirit” (liberty, innovation) while stressing the need for careful, legally grounded rulemaking and continued convening of industry and staff. Panelists (industry, academics, technologists, lawyers) provided a broad set of views across a spectrum from pro-innovation / minimal prior restraints (protect code publication, protect permissionless innovation) to calls for tailored disclosure, activity-based regulation, and bright lines for high-risk activities (custody, money transmission, centralized control).
- Immediate outcome(s): No final rule was adopted. The Chair announced a directive to staff to explore a “conditional exemptive relief” / “innovation exemption” framework to enable on‑chain products to come to market in the U.S. while staff evaluate fit‑for‑purpose rules. The Task Force invited continued public engagement and written input via the SEC crypto web channels.
- Main unresolved questions: precise jurisdictional boundaries (when and which on‑chain activity is a securities transaction), the role of intermediaries vs. truly non‑custodial systems, whether/when code publication is speech protected from regulation, treatment of stablecoins (seize/freeze tradeoffs), and whether disclosure or fraud-based enforcement (or both) is the appropriate regulatory toolset.

3. VIEWS ON TOPICS
(Organized by main topic; viewpoints distilled; where possible, representative quotes included)

Topic: How to define DeFi / DeFi hallmarks
- Views expressed:
  - Panelists emphasized DeFi is best defined functionally: blockchain‑based, open source, self‑custodial/non‑custodial, autonomous code enabling peer‑to‑peer economic transactions (Rebecca, Jill).
  - Some argued DeFi is often “peer‑to‑pool” in practice (Omid, others) and that a spectrum of decentralization exists.
  - Commissioner/Chair framing: DeFi embodies American values but is heterogeneous; definitions should not be the sole determinative factor for regulation.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus on treating decentralization as a spectrum and focusing on activities rather than purely on whether something is labeled “DeFi.”
  - Disagreement on how prescriptive the definition should be and whether a principles‑based vs. technical bright‑line approach is preferable.

Topic: Jurisdiction / when securities laws apply (registration, intermediaries, developers)
- Views expressed:
  - Commissioner Pur stressed First Amendment protections for code publication but recognized developers/operators who exercise control can be regulable (cited EtherDelta/Coburn).
  - Several panelists argued activity‑based analysis (what actors do) is the right approach: custody, execution decisions, fee setting, control of upgrade/admin keys are markers for regulation (Rebecca, Jill, Gabe).
  - Some urged bright lines for licensing regimes that carry criminal penalties (Peter).
  - Others advocated for rulemaking, disclosure regimes or contractual solutions instead of heavy ex ante restraints (Krenshaw, Troy, Gabe).
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus that merely publishing code should not automatically trigger registration.
  - Disagreement on how to operationalize the line where developer/maintainer actions convert speech into regulable conduct (some want clearer bright lines for activities that carry strict liability; others prefer case-by-case/fraud remedies).

Topic: Code as Speech / First Amendment constraints
- Views expressed:
  - Strong advocacy that code publication is protected speech and prior restraints are problematic (Peter, Commissioner Pur).
  - Panelists agreed speech protection does not immunize operational conduct (running a platform, custody) that creates regulated activities.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Broad agreement: First Amendment is relevant; regulators should avoid prior restraints on pure publication. Disagreement remains on where speech ends and conduct/regulated activity begins.

Topic: Risk taxonomy — cyber, system management, illicit finance
- Views expressed:
  - Panelists repeatedly identified three central risks: cyber/technical risk (code/hacks), system management (who can pause/upgrade), illicit finance (money laundering, sanctions).
  - Suggested mitigants: privacy‑enhancing tech, zero‑knowledge proofs, activity‑based detection, better identity tools (Kevin, Michael Mosier, Jill).
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Consensus that risks exist and are different in kind from legacy finance; strong interest in technology-enabled mitigations rather than defaulting to heavy‑handed intermediated approaches.

Topic: Disclosure vs. fraud / enforcement as regulatory tools
- Views expressed:
  - Some (Gabe, panel investors) advocated disclosure of material off‑chain agreements (market maker deals, subsidy deals) and that disclosure can enable markets to price accurately.
  - Others (Eric and others) cautioned disclosure can devolve into burdensome, counterproductive requirements (GDPR analogy; “accept all” fatigue) and that fraud/contract law remedies may be more effective.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - Agreement that transparency about material relationships matters. Disagreement on whether broad mandatory disclosure regime is appropriate / feasible in early, modular DeFi ecosystems, or whether targeted disclosure + fraud enforcement is the better route.

Topic: Stablecoins and seize/freeze tradeoffs
- Views expressed:
  - Concern that custodial stablecoin regulation (seize/freeze abilities) can undermine permissionless DeFi; but such powers are requested by authorities for AML/OFAC reasons.
  - Panelists flagged interaction problems when regulated custodial money (stablecoins) intersects with non‑custodial DeFi protocols.
- Consensus/Disagreement:
  - No consensus on optimal path; panelists emphasized need to analyze tradeoffs and potential systemic impacts.

Topic: Interoperability and modular architecture
- Views expressed:
  - Technologists (Jill) emphasized modern stack is modular (L2s, data availability, privacy layers). Regulators should avoid over-simplistic single-locus approaches and consider multiple components where control might exist.
  - Interoperability is inevitable; better to enable safe interoperability with standards than to force fragmented underground interactions.
- Consensus:
  - Broad agreement interoperability matters and regulator engagement should reflect modularity.

Topic: Role of SEC — what should it do next?
- Views expressed:
  - Chair/Commissioners: SEC should pursue rulemaking, staff guidance, and consider an innovation exemption framework; continue public convenings.
  - Panelists: SEC can be effective in disclosure mandates for things that materially affect securities transactions, scenario planning, and convening; some encouraged SEC to be cautious to avoid chilling innovation.
- Consensus:
  - Strong consensus that continued SEC engagement, public consultation, and careful rulemaking are required.

4. MEETING CONCLUSION
- Overall outcome:
  - No new binding rule announced. The Chair publicly directed staff to explore a “conditional exemptive relief” / “innovation exemption” to enable certain registrants to trial on‑chain products under conditions — a concrete next‑step for staff work.
  - Commissioners voiced support for formal rulemaking (notice and comment) and more public input.
- Explicit action items / next steps:
  - SEC Chair: directed staff to study and report on a conditional exemptive relief framework (innovation exemption) for on‑chain products and services (Chairman’s directive) — transcript statement: “I’ve directed the staff to consider a conditional exemptive relief framework…”
  - SEC Crypto Task Force: continue convenings; solicit written input via SEC crypto webpage (crypto.se.gov / sec.gov crypto task force pages).
  - Staff and Commissioners: engage in scenario planning (as recommended by Kevin) and evaluate where statutory rulemaking vs. staff guidance is necessary.
  - Industry: continue to develop privacy‑enhancing proofs, interoperable technology, and industry disclosure/monitoring practices (L2Beat, security alliance examples cited by panelists).
- Key takeaways:
  - The SEC is signaling a more engagement‑friendly posture but insists on doing rulemaking through proper administrative processes. Practical policy work is moving forward (staff exploration of exemptions and potential rulemaking).
  - Regulators and industry broadly agree on a need for an activity‑based approach, but substantial legal and policy questions remain—particularly around custody, control, and seizure powers for custodial stablecoins.
  - Industry self‑governance and technical mitigations (privacy proofs, zero‑knowledge, activity monitoring) were highlighted as feasible complements to legal/regulatory approaches.

5. KEY POINTS AND MAIN ARGUMENTS / SIGNIFICANT MOMENTS
Important/defining points made during the meeting (paraphrased; transcript quotes included where especially illustrative):

A. Chair/Commissioner statements that set policy frame
- Chair Atkins: emphasized American values, the importance of congressional authority to give force of law, and directed staff to consider an innovation exemption. (Representative quote above.) This was the clearest statement of near‑term policy process.
- Commissioner Krenshaw: urged methodical, notice‑and‑comment rulemaking: “With issues this complex and stakes this high, it’s better to do it right than fast.” [transcript]
- Commissioner Pur: emphasized First Amendment protections and that code publication alone should not be regulated as securities conduct — but noted circumstances where operators/administrators might be regulable (EtherDelta/Coburn example). Representative quote: “I do not believe that we should allow a century old regulatory framework to stifle innovation … We should not automatically fear the future.” [transcript]

Significant moment: Chair’s staff directive to explore “conditional exemptive relief” (innovation exemption). This is a concrete, actionable policy step and the primary meeting “outcome.”

B. Definition / Activities framing
- Panelists pushed an activities-first approach: focus on custody, control, fee setting, order book control, admin keys, and other observable actions rather than pure technology or labels. Rebecca: “I typically define DeFi as a blockchain‑based open software system … self‑custodial … without the need for traditional intermediaries.” [transcript]
- Example enforcement case used to illustrate boundary: EtherDelta / Coburn — Coburn retained admin keys and central server order book; SEC charged causing exchange to violate registration. Commissioner Pur invoked this to demonstrate where federal securities jurisdiction has been applied. Quote: “Coburn … was the only person with the ability to alter the Ether Delta smart contract… the order book sat on a centralized server.” [transcript]
  - (RAG note: EtherDelta/Coburn is a prior SEC enforcement reference — see SEC materials and staff speeches referenced in rag_search results.)

C. Code = speech debate
- Peter and others argued publication of code is protected; regulation of software development risks becoming a prior restraint. Counterpoint: if developers act in ways that functionally create intermediated services (custody, execution), the conduct is regulable.
- Representative quote (Peter): “Yes, we're always going to want to try and craft our regulation … but at a certain point … a right like my right to publish code trumps the risk that that code creates in the world.” [transcript]

D. Risk taxonomy and technological mitigations
- Risk types emphasized: cyber (smart contract vulnerabilities, hacks), system management (pausability, admin keys), illicit finance (money laundering, sanctions).
- Technological mitigants proposed: zero‑knowledge proofs (for eligibility checks without sharing PII), activity‑based detection (bots detection, faster automated risk detection than manual SARs), privacy‑enhancing tech (Kevin, Michael Mosier, Jill).
- Representative quote (Kevin): “There are ways to do this that protects people … zero knowledge proofs that could verify threshold proofs … at transaction speed.” [transcript]

E. Disclosure vs. fraud enforcement
- Investors (Gabe, Michael Jordan) pushed for material disclosure of off‑chain agreements (market maker subsidies, subsidized liquidity) so markets can price incentives accurately.
- Other panelists (Eric, Peter) cautioned that disclosure regimes can become unwieldy and that fraud/negligence remedies should be used where appropriate.
- Representative quote (Michael Jordan): “Tokens are broken … It is more profitable to listen to user demand … We need to create a golden bridge that everyone crosses.” (Argument for predictable, investor‑friendly rules/standards.) [transcript]
- Representative quote (Eric): “Why doesn't requiring Apple to do disclosures make the experience of using an iPhone worse?” (counterpoint arguing that appropriate disclosure can be implemented without hurting user experience). [transcript]

F. Interoperability & modularity
- Jill Gunter urged regulators to recognize complexity of multi‑layer, modular DeFi stacks (L2s, DA layers, privacy layers, interoperability middleware) — “you can’t always point to a single locus of control.” [transcript]

G. Stablecoins & systemic interactions
- Panelists warned that seize/freeze powers at custodial stablecoin issuers have large downstream impacts on non‑custodial DeFi liquidity and operations; this is an unresolved tradeoff.

H. Industry self‑policing examples
- Panelists cited L2Beat (L2 risk dashboard), security alliance white‑hat activity (return of funds), and on‑chain governance (community takeover) as examples of non‑regulatory mitigants and industry progress.
